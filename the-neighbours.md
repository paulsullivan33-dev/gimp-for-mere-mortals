# The Neighbours

GIMP is not the only program that
touches pictures, and several of its
neighbours do jobs it deliberately
does not. An honest map:

## Krita

The painter's program, and the
neighbour readers ask about most.
Krita is free like GIMP and shares the
layer-and-canvas skeleton, but its
heart is the brush: it is built for
drawing and painting from a blank
page — illustration, comics, concept
art — with brush engines, stabilisers,
and animation tools to match. If your
job starts from an empty canvas and a
stylus, Krita is the right tool and
GIMP is the awkward one. If your job
starts from a photograph that needs
fixing, the positions reverse: GIMP's
selection, levels, and repair tools
are the deeper kit. Many mortals keep
both, the way a workshop keeps a saw
and a plane.

## Photoshop

The professional standard, rented by
the month, and the program GIMP is
forever compared to. Photoshop's
advantages are real: polish,
performance on enormous files, the
industry's shared vocabulary and file
habits, and — for print work — native
CMYK editing that chapter 11 explained
GIMP's lack of. Its costs are also
real: the subscription never ends, and
the program is a professional's
cockpit, not obviously kinder to a
beginner than GIMP's. For the jobs in
this guide — repairs, web pictures,
posters — GIMP's tools are sufficient,
which is not faint praise but the
testable claim chapters 4 to 12 made.
Where a workplace runs on Photoshop
files and habits, that gravity wins,
and this guide's concepts transfer
almost whole: layers, masks, and
levels are the same ideas in both
houses.

## The simple editors

Every system ships one — Photos on
macOS and Windows, and a shelf of
small Linux viewers and editors — and
for the smallest jobs they are the
right answer: crop, straighten,
auto-fix, share, done, without
learning what a layer is. Their
ceiling arrives quickly (no layers,
no masks, repairs limited to a slider
the program chose for you), but
honesty requires saying how much of
life lives under that ceiling. Reach
for GIMP when the simple editor says
no — it will, at exactly the moment
the job gets interesting.

## The developers and the librarians

Two neighbours handle photographs
*before and around* the edit rather
than in it. **RAW developers** —
darktable and RawTherapee are the free
names — take the unprocessed data
from a camera sensor and develop it:
exposure, white balance, lens
corrections, the negative become a
picture. That developed picture is
then GIMP's starting material.
**Librarians** — digiKam is the free
stalwart — catalogue thousands of
photographs, tag faces and places,
and find the one you want; GIMP edits
one picture at a time and keeps no
catalogue at all. A serious
photographer's chain is usually all
three: librarian to find, developer
to develop, GIMP to repair and
compose.

## The drawing and layout tools

When the picture is made of shapes
rather than pixels, **Inkscape** (free)
and its commercial cousins draw in
**vectors** — outlines a computer can
enlarge forever without softening,
which is why logos are born there and
chapter 14 sends them there. When the
job is pages — booklets, newsletters,
text in columns flowing around
pictures — **Scribus** (free) is the
page-layout tool, and chapter 9's
posters are the upper limit of what
should be attempted inside an image
editor. Neighbours, all of them, not
rivals: the mortal's skill is knowing
which house the job lives in.
