# Text and Simple Designs

By the end of this chapter you will be
able to make the thing half of all
editing is actually for: a simple
design — a poster, a book cover, a
banner — with a photograph, a headline,
and supporting text arranged in layers
you control. No artistic gift is
assumed; design at this level is mostly
restraint, alignment, and type sizes
chosen on purpose.

## Text lives on its own layer

Choose the **Text** tool, click on the
canvas, and type. GIMP makes a **text
layer**: the words stay *words* —
editable, re-fontable, movable — rather
than becoming painted pixels. Double-
click the layer's thumbnail in the
Layers dialog and you are editing the
sentence again. This is the deepest
practical difference between a design
and a doodle, and it is why the XCF is
the file you keep: the exported poster
has flattened its headline into the
picture forever, while the XCF's
headline can still be corrected at
midnight before printing.

The tool options and the floating
editor give you font, size, colour,
spacing, and alignment. Two habits
carry a beginner design most of the
way: pick **one** font family and use
its bold for headlines and regular for
the rest, and make the headline big
enough to be read from twice as far
away as you think. A design with two
fonts and a clear size ladder looks
deliberate; one with five fonts looks
like a ransom note.

## A poster, start to finish

The fixture: a poster for a village
fête — an invented occasion, a
photograph of a field and tents, a
headline, three lines of detail.

1. **Size first.** **File → New** at
   the size the destination needs — a
   printed A4 poster at 300 pixels per
   inch is 2480 × 3508; a screen banner
   wants its own pixels (chapter 8).
   Designing at final size means every
   later choice is honest.
2. **Background.** Open the photograph
   as a layer (**File → Open As
   Layers**), scale it to cover the
   canvas, and if the headline needs
   quiet room, darken the photo a
   little (chapter 7's levels) or slip
   a translucent rectangle layer behind
   the text — a rectangle selection on
   its own layer, filled with white,
   its opacity turned down to taste.
3. **Headline.** Text tool, the fête's
   name, large, centred, a colour that
   stands honestly against the photo —
   white with a soft dark shadow is the
   classic for a reason. (The shadow is
   the text layer duplicated, coloured
   black, blurred a touch, nudged two
   pixels down and right, and sent
   behind the original: chapter 5's
   whole toolkit in one flourish.)
4. **Details.** A second text layer,
   much smaller: date, place, one line
   about what is on. Left-aligned under
   the headline, in a column the eye
   can follow.
5. **Align like you mean it.** The
   **Alignment** tool and the guides
   (dragged from the rulers at the
   canvas edges) exist so that edges
   share lines on purpose. A design
   where three things start at the
   same invisible vertical reads as
   composed; move layers a pixel at a
   time with the arrow keys to finish.

Export for print as PNG or high-quality
JPEG at full size, and for the web by
chapter 8's routine — same design, two
exports, one XCF.

## The honest edges of design-in-GIMP

Text in GIMP is good and getting
better — GIMP 3.0 improved font
handling and lets text layers carry
outlines and styling more gracefully —
but GIMP remains a picture editor doing
design, not a design program. Posters,
covers, banners, social images, and
simple leaflets sit comfortably inside
it. A forty-page booklet, text that
must flow around shapes in columns, or
print work where the printer demands
exact colour separations belong to
other tools — the page-layout
neighbours in chapter 13 — and chapter
14 says so without apology.

Check: reopen your exported poster at
full size and look only at the text.
Every word is legible against its
background, nothing crowds an edge, the
headline can be read across the room —
and reopening the XCF lets you change
the date in under a minute, because the
words are still words.
